Method and limits

Key point

Articles are the unit; dates are the lunar originals plus a solar conversion; astronomy is computed directly from DE406. Multiply keyword metrics by the precision measured on coded samples. The Sillok records what was written, not what happened — a limit that applies to every frequency metric.

Sources

National Institute of Korean History (Ministry of Education), 'Veritable Records of the Joseon Dynasty — original text' (data.go.kr dataset 15053647, KOGL Type 1): 25 reigns Taejo–Cheoljong, 25 principal editions plus 2 revised, 1 supplemented, 1 supplement-and-corrections edition and the two drafts of the Daily Records of Gwanghaegun; 676 XML files. The Gojong and Sunjong records come from 'Gojong–Sunjong original text' (15053646, KOGL Type 4: no derivatives, non-commercial) and are used only for counts that neither alter nor quote the text (articles, characters, classification, keyword hits). Counted at the article level (biblioData type="T"). The range is therefore Taejo to Sunjong, 1392–1910; the Gojong and Sunjong records appear in the article table without titles (ID, date, character count, classification and link only) and without context excerpts in the keyword table. The sky series (C) covers Taejo–Cheoljong only. Sample coding does include articles from the two records, but in the published coding tables their title, quote and summary are blanked. Reign length is measured as the JDN span between the first and last dated articles, the same rule before and after the 1896 calendar switch.

NIKH, 'Veritable Records — supplementary person data' (15053645): 115,859 persons, 336,255 office-holding rows. Academy of Korean Studies, 'Successful candidates of the civil service examination' (15052752): 15,151 examination passers with name, clan seat and year.

Counting unit

Everything is counted per article: one biblioData type="T" element in the NIKH XML. Of 414,501 articles, 412,769 carry a date. Time series use the principal editions only (the final draft for Gwanghaegun); revised and supplemented editions appear only in the editions series. Characters are the Chinese characters of the article text.

Dates

Sillok dates are lunar. The CE year and lunar month/day are kept as given; solar dates come from korean_lunar_calendar, shown in the Julian calendar before 15 October 1582. From 1 January 1896 (Geonyang 1) the court used the Gregorian calendar and the NIKH XML marks those days 陽曆, so their month and day are taken as given rather than converted (`date_system` column). The conversion was checked astronomically: the 6,642 lunar month-starts of 1392–1928 were compared with DE406 new moons in Seoul local mean time (UT1 + 126.978° E; ΔT from the Stephenson–Morrison–Hohenkerk 2016 model) — 6,463 (97.3%) fall on the same day, 179 one day off, none two or more. Eclipse matching therefore uses a ±1-day window, with the same-day value shown alongside. Of the dated articles, 26 have no solar date because the conversion package rejected the day: 23 fall on the 30th of a month to which the package assigns only 29 days, 2 on a 31st, and 1 has an unknown day (99). Whether these are errors in the source or differences between calendars is left open until a check against the KASI chronological tables. A further 38 articles whose day reads '??' are counted as undated.

Astronomy

JPL DE406 planetary ephemeris read with skyfield. New moons, solar and lunar eclipses and inferior conjunctions of Venus were computed directly; ΔT from the Stephenson–Morrison–Hohenkerk 2016 long-term model. For every new moon the apparent discs of Sun and Moon were tested for overlap above the Seoul horizon (37.57° N, 126.98° E) at 2-minute steps. Apparent radii use 696,000 km for the Sun and 1,737.4 km for the Moon (with 695,700 km one grazing contact on 1528-11-22 drops out, giving 184); the `newmoon_jd` and `jd` columns are TT Julian dates. The 185 eclipses visible from Seoul in 1392–1863 so obtained all appear in the Espenak–Meeus canon, and all 1,125 of its eclipses match a new moon within ±1 day. Lunar eclipses come from skyfield's eclipse finder; visibility is judged by the Moon's altitude at maximum eclipse. Times of day were not compared in this version because of the timekeeping systems of the period.

Keywords and sample coding

Many metrics in series C, D and E select articles by Chinese-character keywords. Keywords also match passages where the characters mean something else ('日食一升' is a daily ration of rice), so for each keyword metric a stratified sample of hits was coded, and its precision with a Wilson 95% interval sits on the reliability card. All 332 eclipse articles were classified by type, and 34 were blind re-coded by another coder. Coding followed a codebook and was done by several language-model coders; every quotation was string-checked against the source. Codebooks and all judgments are published.

Reliability cards

Three kinds. Exact counts are read straight from the source and checked against external figures (Bak & Oh 2015; an independent 2026-09 index). External matches are rates of agreement with astronomical computation. Sampled precision is the share of keyword hits that mean what the metric says; multiply keyword counts by it.

Limits

  • The Sillok records what was written down, not what happened. Frequency metrics (omens, death-penalty mentions, relief) reflect recording practice — the Sejong peak in death-penalty mentions turned out to be sentencing paperwork.
  • NIKH's classification, comment tags and person links are modern editorial work of varying granularity. Person appearance counts are not used as metrics because of linking errors.
  • The roster fill for clan concentration is 91% accurate, and after 1850 the clan of 20% of appointees remains unidentified.
  • The series C lunar match rate (C2) is a lower bound without type classification; G2 in 1.2 gives the values by type.

1.2 — three sources and the sky

National Institute of Korean History, 'Korean History Database — Goryeosa original text' (data.go.kr 15053637, no restriction). All 4,568 articles of the Treatise on Astronomy (fascicles 47–49). NIKH's lunar date, Julian solar date and day ganji are used as given and cross-checked. National Institute of Korean History, 'Goryeosa-jeolyo original text' (data.go.kr, KOGL Type 1). 249 articles hit by the astronomy keywords; dates are monthly, and days restored from the ganji in the first clause are flagged in `date_flag`. National Institute of Korean History, 'Seungjeongwon Ilgi' (data.go.kr 15064218, KOGL Type 1): 297 XML volumes, 104,074 day nodes, 1,792,433 articles. Every weather cell and the 8,520 astronomy-keyword articles are used. The new articles sit in a separate table (articles_ext) so that the 1.1 Sillok counts are unchanged.

Sites and dates. Gaeseong (37.97° N 126.55° E) for the Goryeo sources, Seoul for Joseon. NIKH's Goryeosa solar dates are Julian; re-converting the 4,436 articles after 1000 with the calendar library reproduces 4,435, and the one exception (the eclipse of Gojong 36, 4th month, day 1) is a year typo in the NIKH solar date (1246→1249), corrected. The Ilgi stays lunar to the end; of its 2,747 well-formed '陽曆 m月 d日' notes after 1896 (12 irregular ones excluded), 2,743 agree with the conversion. Day ganji agree with the converted date on 103,870 days; three whole months (1699-2, 1709-11, 1751-11) are shifted by one day — the contemporary almanac started those months a day away from the modern table — and for them the ganji-following date (solar_date_ganji) is used. ΔT before 1392 is a model value with a standard error of about one minute around the year 1000 (Morrison & Stephenson 2004): no effect on day-level matching, but borderline visibility calls can move.

Coding. Solar codebook v2 fixed, before re-coding, the four boundary cases that split the 1.0 review (below-horizon computation → forecast; reporting orders → forecast; ritual preparation → ritual; undated calendrical method → discussion). The lunar codebook uses the same frame plus an after-midnight flag. The slaves keyword was re-coded with a v2 rule naming four contexts (punishment, interrogation, transfer, labor duty) as yes: κ 0.86 on 30 blind articles (v1 0.44), precision 0.68 (v1 0.54). Astronomy keywords and the weather token rule were registered before computing and not adjusted afterwards. Coders were several language models (15 batches plus 3 blind); every quotation was string-checked against the source (check_coding.py). In 1.2.1 the blind verification found a systematic split between coder batches — the reply dispatches sent to the Qing Board of Rites after an eclipse had passed ('日食已過, 禮部回咨') were classed as forecast (reporting order) by some batches and as discussion by others — and an editing rule now places them under discussion (15 solar, 85 lunar articles; the coder's type is kept in type_coder and the coder files are untouched). The blind re-coding pairs (blind_recoding_pairs) and the judgments of the 1.0 precision samples (precision_sillok_samples) are published as well.

Lyu et al. (2024), 'Digitisation of Weather Records of Seungjeongwon Ilgi', Geoscience Data Journal; data: Zenodo doi:10.5281/zenodo.8382243 (CC BY 4.0). (2024), 'Precipitation data in Seoul, Korea during 1778–1907', Scientific Data 11:566; data: Zenodo doi:10.5281/zenodo.11076768 (CC BY 4.0). Ilgi weather category and rain-gauge depth by day. Those two datasets digitized the cell first. This site adds the day-by-day agreement with them and the lists of differences, a routine-entry index and the structure of the gaps. Shares of the cell describe entries in a secretariat form, not Seoul's weather — the comparisons with the rain gauge and with modern observations show why.
