Clan concentration among first appointees to high office

People first appointed as state councillor, minister, vice-minister, inspector-general, censor-general, chief royal secretary, provincial governor or director of the academy, grouped in 50-year bins: top-10 clan share, HHI and effective number of clans.

0.28→0.45top-10 clan share, 1392–1449 → 1800–1849
Key point

Puts a number on the thesis of oligarchic concentration: the top-10 clan share of high office rose from the 17th century to the early 19th and survives a pessimistic lower bound. Citable, provided a clan is understood to be wider than a lineage.

Formula
first appointment year from NIKH office records; clan from the person data, gaps filled from the examination roster (91% fill accuracy). Resampled to 247 per bin with 95% intervals, plus a lower bound treating remaining gaps as distinct clans.
How to read it
Top-10 share rises from 0.28 (1392–1449) to 0.45 (1800–1849), more than among exam passers over the same period (0.22→0.33). The two groups differ in composition, missingness and matching, so the gap is not read as a promotion effect; that would need person-level tracking from examination to office.
Caveats
A clan is wider than a lineage. After 1850, 20% of seats remain unmatched, so the last bin is provisional. The 91.0% fill accuracy is the agreement rate when the same rule is applied to the 2,279 first appointees whose clan is already known; resampling uses seed 20261101, 247 per period, 1,000 draws.
