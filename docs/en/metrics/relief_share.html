Share of famine-relief articles

Share of articles mentioning relief, famine or crop failure, by year, with sample precision.

1.2%of articles have a relief or famine keyword · precision 0.81
Key point

With 81% precision, relief and famine articles point to real crises and relief measures; the peaks in the 1670–71 and 1695–96 famines make the series a first draft of a famine chronology.

Formula
Keyword articles / all articles
How to read it
Check whether peaks appear in the great famines of 1670–71 and 1695–96.
Caveats
Stock phrases used in general argument were coded 'no' in the sample.
Reliability card
sampledSampled precision — share of keyword hits that mean what the metric says, measured on a coded stratified sample

Precision 80.6% (Wilson 95% 71.9%–87.1%) on a coded stratified sample of 103 articles (10 per 50-year period, principal editions). The 2 articles left undecided were counted as 'no'. The codebook and all judgments can be downloaded from the Data page.

Words used on this pageCoding, coder — reading the texts one by one and assigning each a type, or a yes or no, by rules written down beforehand (the codebook). The coder is whoever makes those judgments; on this site, a language model (AI).
95% interval (confidence interval) — the range in which the true rate for the whole plausibly lies, given the rate measured on a sample. With random samples, ranges built the same way again and again contain the true value about 95 times in 100. Other things equal, the smaller the sample, the wider the range.
Wilson — the name of the method used to compute that range; it still works when the sample is small or the rate is near 0% or 100%.
Precision — of the items a rule (a keyword or a string rule) picked out, the share that are right. Higher means fewer false hits. On this site it is mostly measured by coders judging a sample of the items picked out.
Stratified sample — the whole is split into groups (periods, classes and so on) and a set number is drawn at random from each, so that the sample does not bunch up in one group. When the same number is drawn from groups of different size, the rate over the whole sample has to be weighted by group size before it stands for the whole.
